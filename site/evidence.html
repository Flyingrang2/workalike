The evidence

What changed along the way, every claim on the main page and the record behind it, and all 144 test runs.

What changed along the way

We changed the idea on day three

We began by trying to copy how each person differs in style. A colleague covering a desk needs the rules behind the work more than a style. So the product became finding those rules, with a person settling what the records can't. Each person still gets an adapter.

We threw out a round of results

A filter that built the training rows dropped the desk's discount authority and what had already been sent, so the rows never matched what the model sees at work. The adapter results from that round were voided. A new test replays the records through the real loop and checks each prompt against its training row. It fails on the old code.

We wrote a second test

We used the first 24 tickets to shape the method, so they could no longer judge it. The main test is 24 new tickets from a new seed, with the answers hashed before any run. On the first sheet, a person's review changed none of the 10 rules and added 29 answers, and the score moved little (23 and 22 right before, 23 and 23 after). So the product now applies rules as found and asks only the open points.

The automatic training isn't there yet

The automatic training built into the product has run once on a real GPU, and its safety check refused the adapter. The check did its job, but this training has not yet produced an adapter good enough to use. Every LoRA number on this site comes from the practice script.

We tried to break it

Three failure scenarios, three times each. With a broken price tool it asked, 3 of 3. With a rule that doesn't apply it quoted list, 3 of 3. With written rules that conflict, it went ahead without asking, 3 of 3, and took 1.71% off, which neither rule says. That last one isn't solved. We also haven't compared the LoRA with a shortened or retrieved rule book, and the README's limits lists the rest.

How the LoRA was made

  1. A scripted worker handled 60 tickets on the sample desk. Muse Glimmer read those records, proposed 10 rules and listed 30 open points.
  2. One person changed none of the rules and answered 29 of the points, in about 10 to 15 minutes, by their own estimate.
  3. Muse Glimmer worked 173 new tickets from those rules. We kept the 155 that passed the checks, made no failed call, and were right by the generator's answers for those practice tickets. None of them is a test ticket.
  4. Those 155, with the rules taken out, trained a LoRA on one 48GB GPU. Practice took 92 minutes and training 40, about $8 of rented GPU. One step was rerun, so the real bill was about $10.
